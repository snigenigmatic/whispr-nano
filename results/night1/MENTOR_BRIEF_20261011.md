# Capstone evidence brief for the 16 October review

**Team: C. Kaustubh et al. · Updated: 13 October 2026**

**13 October correction:** The complete cloud study now has full, independently rescored predictions. Properly configured Whisper Small scores **60.31% WER** on the 405-clip Hindi replication set and **62.13%** on 396 additional recordings. The earlier HF recipe scores **79.41%** and **74.84%** respectively. This removes much of the apparent advantage of our custom repair. Official Turbo scores **38.55%** and **38.67%**, with a much larger model. These are inference baselines, not trained-model improvements.

The repair is **not accepted as a safe method**. On 100 five-repeat human-word controls, collapsing repeated output reduces Turbo's pooled WER but damages 77 recordings. A zero-duration alignment gate reduces that harm but still fails genuine-repetition preservation. Official Small has no five-word-loop triggers on any of the 801 Hindi clips, so a repair restricted to that trigger cannot improve its score here. We have a reproducible decoder-and-metric audit, not yet a publication-ready new method.

The short-Hindi follow-up is complete: 414 natural short-word clips, 204 constructed repetition inputs and 36 silence controls per condition. Disabling Small's no-speech skipping worsened short-word WER from 125.36% to 149.28%, with no exact-word accuracy gain, and introduced words on 34/36 silence controls versus 0/36. Repeating the input waveform also failed to improve isolated-word accuracy. Those interventions are closed. [Complete short-study evidence](short_hindi_validated_20261013.json).

The fixed-vocabulary diagnostic also completed all 450 speech/silence inputs on a native Modal App. Mean prior-corrected scoring raises short-word exact accuracy from 19.81% to 26.81% (paired difference +7.00 points, 95% CI [4.02, 10.43]), but produces words on 32/36 silence controls versus zero for the original decoder. Its primary accuracy comparison with uncorrected mean likelihood has an interval touching zero, and ordinary summed likelihood has lower WER. Thus the prior correction does not pass the declared improvement gate. This is useful evidence about a narrow isolated-word task, not a new safe ASR method. The earlier aggregate-only HF run was repeated with full artifact export. The new cloud beam5 run reproduces **79.41% WER exactly** on the same 405 recordings and retains all hypotheses, word edits and audio hashes. The earlier local MPS score was **78.38%**; hardware and PyTorch differed, so that small cross-runtime difference remains unexplained. The additional [Lahaja benchmark](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/Lahaja) is now accessible to the team's HF account. Its four released Parquet shards contain **6,152 Hindi clips**, **132 speaker IDs**, **83 districts**, and **18 states**; durations total **12.32 hours**. The fields include speaker ID, native state/district/language, read versus extempore scenario, and verbatim transcript. This makes it a useful *Hindi regional robustness* check, not a Hinglish substitute. We score the `verbatim` field because 3,960 of 6,152 `normalized` rows contain brackets, including inserted glosses; the exact reference policy matters. A fixed first-shard Whisper-small greedy run produced long repetitions (12 of 104 clips had hypotheses at least three times the reference length; 27 clips had WER above 100%). CPU float32 and attention-mask controls reproduced the loops. Five-beam decoding improved the *development* WER from 120.63% to 79.47%, but still left 12 clips above 100% WER and 18 generation-cap hits. The zero-extra-model-compute loop repair scored 73.19% on those development clips before it was frozen for the held-out run. These diagnostics identify a decoder failure, not a clean model-capability comparison. Raw WER also counts spoken number words versus written digits as errors. Sixty-six of 405 held-out `verbatim` references contain bracketed annotation items such as `[uhh]`; a post hoc sensitivity that removes those items changes the paired reduction from 41.94 to 42.94 points, while the literal-reference result remains the headline.

**What this yields for the review:** a source-checked Svarah Indian-English comparison, reproducible MUCS and HiACC baselines, a measured MUCS decoder-policy failure, a sharply defined HiACC script-dependent error pattern, and a paired Lahaja small-model intervention on unseen speakers. The exploratory beam-plus-repair combination is promising but was selected after inspecting a held-out result and cannot be the paper's confirmatory estimate. The next defensible paper question is whether a compact open-ASR policy can suppress *unsupported* repetitions while preserving actually spoken repeats and task-relevant numbers and terms. A public [genuine-repetition benchmark](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Scicom-intl/Whisper-Hallucination) exposes the limitation of our text-only rule: applying it to perfect references would alter **960/1,440** examples and delete **6,480/9,120** words ([audited counterexample](lahaja_loop_repair_positive_control_20261011.json)). That is an oracle structural test, not an observed model false-positive rate, but it rules out presenting the rule as a safe general solution. A [development-only selective beam fallback](SELECTIVE_BEAM_DEV_20261013.md) also failed to beat full beam decoding or the text repair. The paper-grade next step is an acoustic-support gate, evaluated on both real decoder loops and genuinely repeated speech, plus an intervention on Indian-English/Hinglish data that matches the original capstone goal. Sarvam remains an optional comparator after written evaluation-use permission arrives.

The [original Lahaja paper](https://www.alphaxiv.org/pdf/2408.11440) already reports regional weaknesses and failures on named entities and specialized terms. Repeating those observations is a benchmark check, not a new contribution. The publication test must be a *measured intervention* on a small open model, with held-out speakers, term and number-specific accuracy, absent-term false positives, and overall WER reported together.

Nor is number-aware scoring alone new: [The Hidden Cost of Digits](https://www.alphaxiv.org/pdf/2609.21084) studies number normalization's effect on ASR WER, and [mu-bench](https://www.alphaxiv.org/pdf/2609.32082) evaluates form-field transcription against human judgments. The narrow contribution to test here is a low-footprint correction or decoding policy for Indian-accented open ASR, plus evidence that it preserves the actual number or entity value on speakers unseen during policy development.
