# Capstone evidence brief for the 16 October review

**Team: C. Kaustubh et al. · Status: 11 October 2026**

We have completed an auditable public-data ASR pilot rather than another training run. A pinned Whisper large-v3 checkpoint produced predictions for all **3,136 MUCS Hindi–English lecture segments** and **5,176 HiACC adult/child Hinglish clips**. Audio format, corpus hashes, model revision, decode settings, raw predictions, and pooled scores are retained locally. The full evidence and limits are in [RUN_REPORT.md](RUN_REPORT.md). These are baselines; no trained-model improvement or population fairness result is claimed.

The additional [Lahaja benchmark](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/Lahaja) is now accessible to the team's HF account. Its four released Parquet shards contain **6,152 Hindi clips**, **132 speaker IDs**, **83 districts**, and **18 states**; durations total **12.32 hours**. The fields include speaker ID, native state/district/language, read versus extempore scenario, and verbatim transcript. This makes it a useful *Hindi regional robustness* check, not a Hinglish substitute. A fixed first-shard Whisper-small greedy run produced long repetitions (12 of 104 clips had hypotheses at least three times the reference length; 27 clips had WER above 100%). CPU float32 and attention-mask controls reproduced the loops, while the pinned Whisper Turbo recipe above did not. The small-model raw WER is therefore a **decode-quality failure, not a model-capability estimate**. Turbo was qualified on ten clips before the 104-clip run, so the two are not a held-out model comparison. Raw WER also counts spoken number words versus written digits as errors; this needs a separately frozen numeric-fidelity analysis before interpretation.

**What this yields for the review:** reproducible MUCS and HiACC baselines, a measured MUCS decoder-policy failure, a sharply defined HiACC script-dependent error pattern, and an audited Lahaja pilot. The next defensible paper question is whether an inexpensive open-model recipe can improve task-relevant terms and numeric fidelity on authentic Indian speech **without** increasing absent-term insertions or damaging the remaining words. We will first inspect high-error audio and validate term/script labels, then freeze a correction or decoder policy on development speakers and test it on disjoint speakers. Sarvam remains an optional comparator after written evaluation-use permission arrives.

The [original Lahaja paper](https://www.alphaxiv.org/pdf/2408.11440) already reports regional weaknesses and failures on named entities and specialized terms. Repeating those observations is a benchmark check, not a new contribution. The publication test must be a *measured intervention* on a small open model, with held-out speakers, term and number-specific accuracy, absent-term false positives, and overall WER reported together.

Nor is number-aware scoring alone new: [The Hidden Cost of Digits](https://www.alphaxiv.org/pdf/2609.21084) studies number normalization's effect on ASR WER, and [mu-bench](https://www.alphaxiv.org/pdf/2609.32082) evaluates form-field transcription against human judgments. The narrow contribution to test here is a low-footprint correction or decoding policy for Indian-accented open ASR, plus evidence that it preserves the actual number or entity value on speakers unseen during policy development.
