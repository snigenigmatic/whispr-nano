# Paper feasibility and novelty check

Checked 2026-09-09, with sprint updates below. The initial bounded primary-source search launched no experiments or paid services; subsequent experiment execution was separately authorized. This is not an exhaustive literature review or an assurance of acceptance.

## Initial feasibility judgment

A carefully scoped replication/update with a complete manuscript and reproducibility package was a plausible three-hour target because this repository already had transcripts and inference implementations. At the start, runners needed portability fixes, including removal of their dependency on the absent `ast_asr` package. A promise of a publishable or accepted paper was not defensible. Compute alone does not resolve prior art, comparison validity, speaker dependence, or claim strength. The appropriate target was a short empirical paper candidate whose conclusions could change when the analysis was repaired.

## Prior art that changes the framing

- **The original Svarah paper already audits accents across systems.** Javed et al., Interspeech 2023, evaluate six open systems and two commercial systems. Table 2 reports WER by 19 native-language accents. Section 5 specifically identifies poor performance for Assamese, Bodo, and Nepali speakers and compares error patterns across models. Consequently, the team notes' claims that the accent axis is untouched, or that a multi-system Svarah accent audit is itself new, must be withdrawn. This is verified conference publication, not merely a preprint. [Proceedings paper, Table 2 and §5](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2023/javed23_interspeech.pdf)
- **Significance-tested ASR fairness is established methodology.** ASR-FAIRBENCH uses mixed-effects Poisson regression and a fairness-adjusted score on a stratified Fair-Speech sample from U.S. participants. Its methodology is not a contribution this project can claim. It is verified in the Interspeech 2025 proceedings. The local independent-utterance Poisson GLM is also not equivalent to its mixed-effects model. [Proceedings paper](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2025/rai25_interspeech.pdf); [local implementation](../audit/svarah_fairness_audit/assemble.py)
- **Indian clinical ASR auditing has direct precedent.** ASR Under the Stethoscope compares eight systems, including Sarvam, across Kannada, Hindi, and Indian English, examining role and gender disparities. SamaVaani adds a debiasing method. Both arXiv works exist, but this check found no primary evidence establishing their peer-review acceptance. The receipts pack's statement that the clinical audit “got through peer review” is unsupported and should become “appeared as a preprint.” [Clinical audit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10967); [SamaVaani](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26901)
- **A close public competing artifact exists.** The first-party `theshivam7/indian-asr-bench` repository describes nine models on three corpora including Svarah, paired cluster bootstrap with Holm correction, text-normalization sensitivity, and committed transcripts. It names Shivam Sharma and supervisor Changsong Liu at NTU. It is a public repository, not a verified peer-reviewed publication; this check did not establish its first-release date or audit its numeric claims. Its overlap means generic “modern models + reproducibility + bootstrap” is weak novelty. Its summary explicitly says Svarah lacks public speaker IDs and uses recording clusters instead, which needs independent verification in our loader. [Repository](https://github.com/theshivam7/indian-asr-bench); [results and limitations](https://github.com/theshivam7/indian-asr-bench/blob/main/SUMMARY.md)
- **Sarvam motivates an update but does not establish firstness.** Its current Saaras V3 page describes evaluation on Svarah and accent capability but the text of that section supplies no subgroup table. That supports the value of an independent subgroup analysis, not a claim that nobody else has performed one. [Official Saaras V3 page](https://www.sarvam.ai/blogs/asr)

## Narrow contributions worth testing

1. **A 2026 replication of accent-disparity findings with an India-focused commercial recognizer and newer open models.** Use the identical utterance panel, report each native-language group's sample support, and test whether reduced overall error corresponds to reduced absolute and relative disparities. Position Saaras V3 and the exact tested versions as the empirical extension. Do not call this the first Indian-accent fairness audit or infer why a proprietary system has a gap.
2. **A reliability study of the subgroup conclusions themselves.** Quantify whether conclusions persist across consistent normalization, aggregation choices, and uncertainty procedures that reflect the available clusters. An explicitly reported change or disappearance of the current “six structural gaps” headline can be a useful result. This is potentially stronger when applied to subgroup claims and commercial-versus-open comparisons; generic normalization sensitivity already overlaps the competing artifact. Novelty of this narrower combination remains unverified.

## Publication-level risks to resolve before writing conclusions

- The existing 450-clip family-balanced Saaras result and 2,500-clip open-model results do not support a like-for-like overall ranking. Matching the cohort is mandatory. [Current audit documentation](../audit/svarah_fairness_audit/README.md)
- The implemented taxonomy represents Sino-Tibetan with Bodo only. Claims about the whole language family would overgeneralize from this sample; native language is itself only an accent proxy. [Sampling taxonomy](../audit/svarah_fairness_audit/fetch_stratified.py)
- Existing p-values model utterances independently. Multiple clips from the same speakers, unequal group sizes, overdispersion, and multiple comparisons can materially affect inference. Speaker IDs must be verified rather than inferred from recording names. Report effect sizes and intervals; statistical significance does not establish a causal or population-wide “structural” inequity. [Local test](../audit/svarah_fairness_audit/assemble.py)
- Number formatting is already a documented Qwen scoring issue. A symmetric normalization sensitivity check is necessary before substantive model comparisons. [Current audit caveat](../audit/svarah_fairness_audit/README.md)
- A single small existing benchmark can support bounded empirical replication. It cannot establish robustness across Indian English populations or new clinical/deployment settings within this sprint. An additional condition is useful only after cohort and scoring validity are repaired.

No venue acceptance, exhaustive absence of competing work, new methodological contribution, or model superiority is established by this note.

## Sprint update: positioning and inference sources

The authorized sprint's primary design now generates fresh predictions from all five open models on the archived Saaras panel of 450 utterances, while retaining the historical 2,500-utterance open panel as a sensitivity analysis. The panels overlap by 156 utterances and must not be described as independent replications. The explicit mapping has 14 Indo-Aryan native-language labels, four Dravidian labels, and Bodo; both panels cover all 19 labels. [Frozen protocol and recorded amendment](../paper/analysis/PROTOCOL.md)

Two foundational primary references strengthen the methods and further limit novelty claims. Liu and Peng established blockwise-bootstrap ASR comparisons specifically to preserve dependence between related speech observations. Liu, Veliche, and Peng developed mixed-effects Poisson fairness models before ASR-FAIRBENCH, addressing nuisance covariates and unobserved speaker heterogeneity. Neither block resampling nor speaker-aware fairness inference is new here. [Interspeech 2020 paper](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2020/liu20c_interspeech.pdf); [ICASSP 2022 publication](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICASSP43922.2022.9747654); [author manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.09061)

Speaker availability requires careful language. The current pinned Hugging Face schema has no speaker identifier, while the original project README describes a `meta_speaker_stats.csv` containing one. A public issue reports the missing column. The appropriate claim is therefore about the release actually verified in this audit, not an unqualified claim that speaker metadata have never existed. [Current dataset schema](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/Svarah/blob/ebbf7777fe771490696a3f7b007097606fa8c924/README.md); [original repository documentation](https://github.com/AI4Bharat/Svarah); [reported missing-ID issue](https://github.com/AI4Bharat/Svarah/issues/4)

The strongest empirical angle emerging from the first reanalysis is the interaction between normalization, system ranking, and subgroup disparity. On the same historical 2,500 utterances, Qwen's apparent 4.61-point deficit relative to Whisper-small under minimal scoring becomes a 0.05-point difference in the opposite direction under Whisper English normalization, while Qwen's Bodo-versus-others gap grows. This supports a measurement-reliability case study; it does not establish Qwen superiority. Final numbers and inferential qualifications belong in the finalized manuscript and machine-readable results. [Analysis outputs](../paper/results/analysis.json)

## Further boundary check during the sprint

Full Svarah coverage, normalization effects, and high-error-tail diagnostics already appear in the competing artifact. Its Qwen checkpoint is 1.7B; our 0.6B checkpoint, Distil-large-v3, and Saaras V3 extend that model set. Its Whisper results use the native implementation's temperature fallback, so numerical differences from our frozen greedy Hugging Face configuration are not direct reproductions. The benchmark artifact is cited as a public repository, with no claimed venue or release date. [First-party results and decoding description](https://github.com/theshivam7/indian-asr-bench/blob/main/SUMMARY.md)

The original Whisper paper's Appendix C already motivates number, contraction, and spelling normalization and warns that the procedure can yield unintended outputs. Demonstrating that normalization matters is therefore established background. The narrower contribution is quantifying how particular subgroup contrasts, aggregate rankings, and dependence assumptions interact in this audit. [Original text-standardization account](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.04356v1#A3)

The completed Matched-450 comparison falsifies the previous all-six Bodo-worst headline: Small has a negative Bodo-minus-others point estimate under both normalizers. One short Tamil clip contributes 200 of 621 Minimal errors through repetitive decoding. All raw hypotheses remain in the primary result, and the decoder configuration is a limitation. This observation motivated an explicitly post hoc full-corpus extension, but the user subsequently prioritized faster completion and stopped that extension. The delivered paper uses only completed Matched-450 and Open-2500 results; no partial extension results enter its claims. [Verified matched results](../paper/results/analysis.json); [amended protocol](../paper/analysis/PROTOCOL.md)
